# What biological replay prioritization supports

Research note, 2026-10-22. This is a targeted, primary-source synthesis, written after the completed Fashion-MNIST evaluation. It adds no experiment, training, inference, or biological validation.

Biological replay is selective, and its content depends on the task, experience and brain state. The evidence does not identify a universal priority score. A uniform reservoir, supervised high-loss selection and maximally interfered retrieval (MIR) are engineering choices, with different relationships to that evidence.

This note extends the existing [neural-motif memo](https://github.com/EastonSell/neurofunctional-lab/blob/c934858e1c668df9a5289b1b8b98e13f407a6b08/docs/neural_motifs.md) and [study source table](https://github.com/EastonSell/neurofunctional-lab/blob/c934858e1c668df9a5289b1b8b98e13f407a6b08/studies/replay-selection-fashion-mnist/source/docs/sources.md). It adds fuller inspection of the original complementary-learning-systems and gain–need accounts, recent human electrophysiology, and evidence limiting a simple planning interpretation.

## Evidence-to-implementation map

“Tested” below means implemented in this classifier study, not validated in a brain. Source numbers refer to the precise reading locations below.

| Biological claim or theoretical proposal | Exact evidence | Computational abstraction | Implementation already tested | Mismatch and limits |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Rapid acquisition can coexist with gradual integration through interleaved reinstatement. | McClelland et al. [1]: original connectionist theory and simulations, especially the interleaved-learning and reinstatement sections. | Retain episodes separately from a parametric learner, then revisit them. | Exact features, labels and IDs in a 200-item reservoir; replay updates one 196→32→10 MLP. | No second learned memory system, neural retrieval, pattern completion or hippocampal–cortical dynamics. Reservoir replacement is an ML sampling policy, not a demonstrated biological storage mechanism. This study compares selectors over identical storage, not replay versus no replay. |
| Weak memories can receive more reactivation. | Schapiro et al. [2], Results/Figs. 3–5: in 24 participants, weaker prior item memory correlated with more rest reactivation; later reactivation predicted later memory. Across-session improvement related to replay in the sleep group. | Current error as a rough weakness signal. | High-loss selects the largest current supervised cross-entropies within matched class quotas. | Human memory strength was not classifier loss. fMRI template reactivation does not directly resolve ripple sequences. Correlation does not establish the selection rule or its causal benefit. |
| Useful access can depend jointly on benefit and future relevance. | Mattar & Daw [3], Methods Eqs. 4–6: expected return improvement decomposes into gain × need. Zhou et al. [4], Figs. 4–6: ripple-centered activity and cortical reactivation covaried with model-derived priority in a human RL task. | Gain: improvement from an update; need: expected future use. | MIR instead ranks the loss increase caused by a virtual incoming-data update. No reward, transition model, policy-improvement gain or future-need estimate was present. | Threatened accuracy is not expected benefit from replay. Equal class quotas constrain cross-class allocation. The 2018 account is normative, not an efficient retrieval mechanism. The 2026 recordings do not prove neural multiplication of gain and need, and did not manipulate ripples. |
| Experience influences later sleep replay through more than a single “weakness” variable. | Huelin Gorriz et al. [5], Results/Figs. 3, 6–7 and Discussion: four rats, manipulated lap counts and re-exposure; repetition and familiarity had different relationships to sleep replay, and cumulative awake replay predicted later sleep replay. | Separate exposure, familiarity and prior reactivation from learning error. | Replay frequencies were logged, but no familiarity score, awake-to-sleep stage or persistent reactivation-based priority was implemented. | A logged repetition count is not a biological familiarity measure. The predictive awake–sleep relationship does not establish that awake replay causally sets sleep priorities. |
| Replay can contribute to online planning through recurrent state changes. | Jensen et al. [6], Results “Rollouts improve the policy…” and Methods “Planning”: a meta-RL model uses simulated trajectories; human behavior and rodent replay analyses constrain the account. | Control when to simulate; integrate imagined trajectories into a recurrent decision state. | The MLP receives stored images and changes weights by SGD; it has no trajectory simulator or recurrent decision state. | A virtual gradient is a hypothetical parameter update, not an imagined action sequence. This model-based theory does not assign a biological responsibility to our hidden layer. |
| Replay need not describe the next action. | Gillespie et al. [7], Results/Figs. 5–7: in four rats, replay favored previous goals and nonrecent locations and did not consistently predict subsequent choice. | Distinguish memory maintenance from immediate action planning. | Final classifier retention was measured; no action-choice or sequence-content prediction was tested. | This task-specific counterexample limits “all replay plans the next move.” It does not rule out planning contributions in other tasks or establish why a classifier selector succeeds or fails. |

## What the completed experiment actually found

The [locked report](https://github.com/EastonSell/neurofunctional-lab/blob/c934858e1c668df9a5289b1b8b98e13f407a6b08/studies/replay-selection-fashion-mnist/results/evaluation-report-public.md) and [numerical summary](https://github.com/EastonSell/neurofunctional-lab/blob/c934858e1c668df9a5289b1b8b98e13f407a6b08/studies/replay-selection-fashion-mnist/results/evaluation-summary-v2.json) retain the primary result: MIR minus high-loss final macro accuracy was **+0.6265 percentage points**, paired-seed 95% bootstrap interval **[−0.359525, +1.5330375] pp**. The prespecified mean ≥1 pp and lower-bound >0 criterion was not met. This is inconclusive, not equivalence or evidence of no effect.

Uniform replay reached 72.1355%, MIR 70.8425%, high-loss 70.2160%, and useful-compute uniform 75.3325%. These secondary comparisons are descriptive. Useful-compute uniform also used more updates/presentations and a separately selected learning rate; its result does not isolate selection or establish wall-time efficiency.

The prespecified selected-minus-shuffled old-class loss audit was **+0.078770 for MIR**, adverse to the proposed protection interpretation. It used sequential fresh-then-replay branches, whereas training used joint fresh-plus-replay batches. It is not a causal decomposition of deployment updates. Ranking MIR's own virtual-harm target correctly is an implementation check, not independent mechanistic confirmation. The 8,000 audits are not 8,000 independent experimental subjects.

Greater replay coverage, repeated hard examples, or differences between the audit and learner could be proposed explanations. Their causal roles were not established. This literature synthesis is retrospective; none of its additional interpretations becomes a prespecified finding.

## Algorithmic provenance

Aljundi et al. [8], §3.1, already introduced the virtual-update loss-increase rule, reservoir memory and candidate subsampling. The study uses their first MIR score, not the historical-best-loss variant. Matched class quotas and compute controls are study-specific changes.

Storage and retrieval are distinct: reservoir admissions were uniform over observed items, while the uniform retrieval control sampled within shared class-balanced quotas. Neither policy is a measured biological rule.

Schaul et al. [9], §§3.2–3.4, use temporal-difference error, stochastic prioritization and importance-sampling correction. Our deterministic high-cross-entropy selector is not that algorithm. Neither ML method establishes a neural implementation. No correspondence between an artificial layer and a brain region was tested.

## One falsifiable next prediction, proposed only

**Future need should add value beyond estimated update benefit when future use changes independently of stored experience.**

A separate sequential-decision experiment could hold stored transition histories fixed, vary future start-state frequencies, and compare estimated gain × estimated need with gain alone, TD-error and uniform controls. All policies would receive the same context information. Use a learned, imperfect model rather than evaluator-provided true gains, and match model capacity, readable memory and total useful compute, including priority-estimation costs. A shuffled-need control would test whether correct need-to-state alignment matters.

The prediction is higher return on independently held-out trajectories for gain × need than gain alone under the changed start distribution. A paired interval wholly above zero would support that bounded prediction; an interval wholly at or below zero would contradict it; an interval crossing zero would remain inconclusive. Freeze the task, estimators, effect threshold, seeds, analysis and resource gates before execution, preserving all outcomes and failures. This is an unexecuted proposal, and the now-inspected Fashion-MNIST test cannot become unseen evidence again.

Even a positive result would establish an algorithmic benefit, not a brain-layer mapping. Biological validation would additionally require independently held-out neural or behavioral predictions that distinguish competing accounts.

## Primary sources and inspected locations

All checked 2026-10-22 through primary publisher pages, author-hosted manuscripts or original arXiv methods. These are paraphrases; no paper text or figures are redistributed. This is not a systematic review.

1. **McClelland, McNaughton & O'Reilly (1995)**, *Why There Are Complementary Learning Systems in the Hippocampus and Neocortex*. Psychological Review 102:419–457, July 1995. [Author-hosted full paper](https://stanford.edu/~jlmcc/papers/McCMcNaughtonOReilly95.pdf), [indexed date/DOI](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7624455/). Inspected “Discovery of Shared Structure Through Interleaved Learning,” “Catastrophic Interference,” and “Reinstatement and Consolidation of Hippocampal Memories in the Neocortical System.” Theory/simulations, not a new biological perturbation experiment.
2. **Schapiro et al. (2018)**, *Human hippocampal replay during rest prioritizes weakly learned information and predicts memory performance*. Nature Communications 9:3920, **2018-09-25**. [Full paper](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-018-06213-1). Inspected Results “Session 1 correlation between memory and subsequent replay,” “Session 2 correlation between replay and subsequent memory,” “Correlation between replay and memory change across sessions,” and Methods “Participants”/“Replay analysis.”
3. **Mattar & Daw (2018)**, *Prioritized memory access explains planning and hippocampal replay*. Nature Neuroscience 21:1609–1617, online **2018-10-22**. [Author-hosted full manuscript](https://www.princeton.edu/~ndaw/md18.pdf), [indexed date/DOI](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30349103/). Inspected Results and Methods, especially expected value of backup Eq. 4, gain Eq. 5 and need Eq. 6. Gain depends on policy improvement, not simply prediction-error magnitude. The introduction explicitly limits the theory's process-level interpretation.
4. **Zhou et al. (2026)**, *Human hippocampal ripples prioritize model-based learning*. Neuron 114:3111–3125.e7, online **2026-04-09**, issue **2026-08-19**. [Full paper](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0896627326002114), DOI 10.1016/j.neuron.2026.03.020. Inspected Results “Hippocampal ripples encode the priority of non-local paths,” “Cortical reactivation of high-priority paths during hippocampal ripple events,” Figs. 4–6, STAR Methods “Behaviour Modelling” Eqs. 9–11, participant details and Discussion limitations. There were 34 epilepsy patients, 31 with hippocampal contacts and 11 included in reactivation analysis after above-chance localizer decoding. Need used arm frequencies of 17%, 33% and 50%; its gain proxy includes prediction error and value of information, unlike the 2018 exact policy-gain definition. Clinically determined, sparse macroelectrode coverage limits layer/subfield specificity; residual pathological or gamma contamination remains possible. Converging end states also prevent fully separating model-based prediction-error effects from the primary reward response.
5. **Huelin Gorriz, Takigawa & Bendor (2023)**, *The role of experience in prioritizing hippocampal replay*. Nature Communications, **2023-12-09**. [Full paper](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-023-43939-z), DOI 10.1038/s41467-023-43939-z. Inspected Fig. 1 for protocol, Results/Fig. 3 for repetition and familiarity, Fig. 6 for cumulative awake replay, Fig. 7 for the awake–sleep relationship, Discussion and Methods “Behavioral protocol.”
6. **Jensen, Hennequin & Mattar (2024)**, *A recurrent network model of planning explains hippocampal replay and human behavior*. Nature Neuroscience 27:1340–1348, online **2024-06-07**. [Full paper](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41593-024-01675-7). Inspected Results “Rollouts improve the policy of the RL agent,” Methods “Planning”/Algorithm 2, and Discussion “Neural mechanisms of planning and decision-making.” Rapid within-task adaptation in this account can occur through hidden-state changes at fixed trained weights.
7. **Gillespie et al. (2021)**, *Hippocampal replay reflects specific past experiences rather than a plan for subsequent choice*. Neuron 109:3149–3163.e6, online **2021-08-26**, issue **2021-10-06**. [Full author manuscript](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8497431/). Inspected Results “Subsequent arm choice is not biased by replay of future arm, current goal, or previous goal,” “Previous goal replay persists through multiple trial blocks,” Figs. 5–7, Discussion and subject details.
8. **Aljundi et al. (2019)**, *Online Continual Learning with Maximally Interfered Retrieval*, NeurIPS 2019. [Original methods §3.1, v3](https://arxiv.org/html/1908.04742v3#S3.SS1), [version history](https://arxiv.org/abs/1908.04742v3): first submitted **2019-08-11**, inspected version **2019-10-29**.
9. **Schaul et al. (2016)**, *Prioritized Experience Replay*, ICLR 2016. [Original methods §§3.2–3.4, v4](https://arxiv.org/html/1511.05952v4#S3), [version history](https://arxiv.org/abs/1511.05952v4): first submitted **2015-11-18**, inspected version **2016-02-25**.
